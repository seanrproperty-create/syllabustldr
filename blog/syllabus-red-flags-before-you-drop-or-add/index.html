Syllabus Red Flags: Course Policies Worth Questioning Before You Drop or Add

8 min read • Reviewed by the SyllabusTLDR academic advising team

Add/drop week gives students a short window to back out of a course before it's locked in, but most students spend that window comparing course descriptions and professor ratings instead of the syllabus itself. In our review of the syllabi behind thousands of courses, three specific policy types show up disproportionately often in the classes students later describe as the hardest of the semester — not because the material was harder, but because the policy left no room to recover from one bad week. None of the three are unusual or improper; they're standard tools professors use to keep a course moving. The issue is only ever timing: catching them while a schedule change is still free costs nothing, and catching them in week seven costs a grade.

None of these three are deal-breakers on their own. What matters is catching them before the drop deadline, while there's still a choice, instead of discovering them the week they actually apply.

Mandatory Attendance With No Stated Exceptions

Most attendance policies build in some slack — two free absences, a doctor's note exception, a lowest-attendance-week drop. The ones worth flagging are the ones that state a hard number with nothing listed underneath it: "Any absence beyond two will result in a full letter grade deduction," full stop. That's not automatically unreasonable, but it means a single bad week — illness, a family emergency, a job interview — carries the same academic cost as skipping class to sleep in. If a policy reads that way, it's worth emailing the professor directly during add/drop week to ask how documented emergencies are actually handled in practice, since syllabi often understate what a professor will flexibly apply once a student actually needs it.

No-Drop Exam Policies

A "no-drop" policy means every exam counts toward the final grade with no lowest score excluded — no safety net if one exam goes badly because of a scheduling conflict, an off day, or content that didn't click in time. Courses with three or more exams and a no-drop policy put a lot of weight on a single bad morning. That's not a reason to avoid the course on its own, but it is a reason to check how the exam dates land against everything else on the calendar that semester — a no-drop policy landing in the same week as two other high-stakes exams is a materially bigger risk than the identical policy in a lighter week. It's worth cross-checking exam dates against the rest of your course load specifically during add/drop week, while switching sections or swapping a course is still an option, rather than after the schedule has already collided.

Three Clauses Worth a Second Look

Clause What it actually means What to do about it
"No exceptions" attendance cap One bad week costs the same as habitual absence Ask the professor how documented emergencies are handled in practice
No-drop exam policy Every exam counts — no safety net for one bad day Check it against your other courses' exam weeks before committing
"Grades may be curved at the instructor's discretion" The final scale isn't known until the semester ends Ask what previous semesters' curves have actually looked like

Vague Curving Language

"Grades may be curved at the instructor's discretion" is the single most common ambiguous clause we see, and it means exactly what it says: nobody, including the professor, can state in week one what an 85% will translate to in December. That's not automatically a problem, but it does mean a student can't calculate standing precisely the way they can in a course with a fixed, published scale — the kind we break down in our guide to how weighted grading actually works. If curving is discretionary, the more useful question isn't "will you curve" — it's "what did the curve look like the last two semesters," which gets an actual number instead of a maybe.

Why the Combination Matters More Than Any One Clause

A single strict clause is manageable on its own — most students can work around one no-exceptions policy or one no-drop exam schedule. The combination is where the real risk sits: a course with mandatory attendance and a no-drop exam policy leaves no room to absorb either a missed class or a bad exam day, which means the semester has to go close to perfectly to avoid a grade hit. When both show up in the same syllabus, that's the strongest signal to actually ask the follow-up questions rather than assume it'll probably be fine, since "probably fine" is exactly what a strict policy is designed to not allow for.

Pro Tip: If you're going to ask a professor about any of these three clauses, ask before the add/drop deadline, not after. The same question in week one reads as due diligence; asked in week six, after a policy has already cost a grade, it reads as damage control.

Checking All Three Before the Deadline

None of these clauses need a long conversation to check — usually one specific email covers all three at once, and the exact subject-line structure that gets a fast reply is in our guide to emailing a professor. Before sending anything, though, it's worth pasting the syllabus into SyllabusTLDR first: it surfaces the attendance policy, exam weighting, and grading language in seconds, so the email that goes out is a specific follow-up question instead of one asking the professor to repeat something the syllabus already states. Running that check across every syllabus during add/drop week, rather than only the course that feels risky, is what actually catches the clauses that would otherwise go unnoticed until they matter.
